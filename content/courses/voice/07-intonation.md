Intonation is the melody of speech: where your pitch goes up and down, which words you lean on, and where you pause. A common trap while practising is going flat, because you're concentrating so hard on the note. This chapter puts the movement back.

**Lean on a word.** The important word in a sentence usually goes a little higher and a little longer. "I didn't say _she_ took it" means something different from "I didn't say she _took_ it". Try it.

**Glide inside words.** Let your pitch slide within a word instead of jumping between flat notes: a long, curved "so-o lovely". This is one of the easiest ways to sound more expressive.

**Exaggerate, then dial back.** Read a line like a children's storyteller, way over the top. Then say it normally. The normal version usually keeps a little of the movement, which is exactly what you want.

**Endings can fall.** Not every question needs to rise and not every statement needs to fall. Let the meaning choose. A confident falling ending is fine.

**Listen back.** Does it sound like what you meant? Does the pitch line move, or is it a straight ruler? A wavier line isn't automatically better; it just shouldn't be stuck.

## melody

It was the first warm morning of spring, and the whole street seemed to wake up at once.

> Your check-in: read it like you're telling a friend. Lean on the words that paint the picture.

## everyday-meaning

Hi, good morning. How are you?

> Say it once curious, once warm and familiar. Listen for how your pitch moves differently.

## storyteller

Once upon a time, a tiny cat found a door that had never been opened before.

> Go full storyteller first, then read it normally. Keep a bit of the movement in the normal version.

## gentle-news

I'm so sorry, the café is closed today, but they're open again tomorrow.

> A bit of disappointing news, said kindly. Let "sorry" dip and "tomorrow" lift a little.

## question

Oh, really? Are you coming too?

> First genuinely surprised, then just checking you heard right. Listen to how the endings differ.

## emphasis

I didn't say she took it. I said she borrowed it.

> Lean on "took" and "borrowed" so the contrast is obvious. Go higher, longer or slower on them; any of those works.

## exaggerate

Could I get a coffee, please? Thank you so much!

> Way over the top first, like a cartoon. Then normal. Keep the bit of sparkle you liked.

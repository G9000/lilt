Resonance is the size of the space your voice sounds in: the tube from your vocal folds up through your throat and mouth to your lips. A bigger space gives a bigger, darker sound; a smaller space gives a smaller, brighter one. Listeners use it alongside pitch, and it can tip the balance: a voice in a small space can sound feminine at a fairly low pitch, and a high voice in a big space can still sound masculine. It often takes a while to feel like yours, so small changes are normal, and there's no deadline.

**What makes the space smaller.** Three things, working together. Your voice box (larynx) rises a little in your throat. Your tongue sits higher and further forward. Your lips spread slightly, as in a small smile. Each one makes the tube shorter or narrower. You won't hold any of them in place: you'll find sounds that make them happen, then keep the feel.

**Feel it with a pant.** Pant like a big dog, slow and deep: "hhuh, hhuh". Then like a tiny dog, quick and light: "hhih, hhih". For the tiny dog your voice box comes up and the space feels small. That's the move. A fingertip resting on the bump at the front of your neck may feel it rise; just notice it, don't press.

**Find it in a whisper.** Whisper "ee" and slide it from dark to bright and back, like a kettle starting to whistle. A whisper has no pitch to fight, so you can move only the space. The bright end is a small space. If whispering takes effort, stay with the tiny-dog pant.

**Add your voice.** Keep that small-space feel and add an easy voice: a toy dog's "wa", then an "ee". The pitch will want to climb with it; that's normal at first.

**Separate it from pitch.** Then teach them to move apart. Hold one easy note and make only the space smaller, then bigger again. Next, keep the space small and let the pitch drop a little. In Lilt's recorder, the pitch line stays level while the darker–brighter strip moves.

**Use it in words.** Hum, open into a small-space "ee", then say "Morning! How are you?" Your mouth still changes shape for every vowel; what stays is the small space behind them. Words full of "ee", "i", "m" and "n" are easiest. "Oo" and "aw" are the hardest to keep small.

**Listen back fairly.** Compare like with like: the same vowel, a similar pitch and loudness, the same mic distance. Lilt compares each held vowel with your first take of it, and while you record in this chapter the strip shows whether the sound is brighter or darker than when the take began. Both are rough: on test sounds the comparison was off by up to about a semitone either way, neither has been tested on real voices, and a different mic or distance can move them. Trust your ears first.

**Keep it gentle.** Smaller is not tighter. Don't hold a swallow, push on your throat or squeeze to keep your voice box up. If your neck, jaw or tongue tightens, or your voice turns pinched, thin or nasal, make the change smaller and reset with a yawn-sigh or the straw from the warm-ups. An easy small space you can keep for a minute beats a strong one that lasts a word.

## small-dog

wa · wa · wa

> A toy dog's bark: small, light and easy, with your voice box coming up a little on its own. Then a big dog's "wa" to hear the contrast, and back to the toy dog. If it pinches, make it softer, not stronger.

## whisper-siren

eee ↗ ↘ · eee

> Whisper an "ee" that slides from dark to bright and back. At the bright end, add an easy voice for a short "ee". If whispering takes effort, use the toy-dog "wa" instead. A whisper has no pitch for Lilt to track.

## bright-slide

eee · eee · eee

> One easy note, three ees: your usual space, a smaller one, then your usual again. Keep the pitch level and change only the space: in the recorder, the pitch line stays put while the strip moves. Can you hear the difference without looking?

## forward-hum

mmmmmmm

> Hum with your tongue resting forward and a small smile. A buzz on your lips or face usually means easy, efficient voicing; it isn't resonance itself. Listen for a smaller, brighter sound.

## m-vowels

mmmee · mmmaa · mmmoo

> Open the hum into each vowel and keep the space behind it small. "Oo" will always be darker than "ee"; keep your tongue forward even there.

## ee

eeeeee

> Your check-in: an easy "ee" as in "see", about four seconds, in the smallest space that feels easy today. Next time, play it beside this one, at a similar pitch and loudness so the comparison is fair.

## bright-morning

Morning! How are you?

> Toy-dog "wa", then hum into an easy "ee", then the greeting. Compare with an ordinary take. The vowels change as you talk; the small space behind them stays.

## m-words

many · maybe · mellow · morning · lemon

> Lean into each m for a moment: it gives an easy, buzzy start. Then open into the word with the space still small.

## giggle

eye · air · ear

> Optional: let a small, easy giggle lead into the words. A giggle tends to bring your voice box up on its own; keep that feel into each word. Skip it if it makes you tense.

## twang

nyah · nyah · nyeh

> A bratty-kid "nyah", bright and a little brassy, at an easy pitch. It narrows the space just above your vocal folds. Keep it from going nasal or pinched, and skip it if it feels tight.

## ah

aaaaaa

> An easy "ah" as in "father", about four seconds, with the space as small as stays easy. "Ah" is naturally darker than "ee", so only compare it with your earlier "ah".

## oo

oooooo

> An easy "oo" as in "food". Lips round for "oo", but keep your tongue a little forward and the space small. Compare only with your earlier "oo".

## note-and-bright

mmm… Morning! How are you?

> Two cues, one short line: hum your note, then keep the small space as the words start. If one slips, go back to just one.

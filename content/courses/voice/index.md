# Hey you, welcome in

Pull up a chair. This is a small, quiet room for working on your voice: a few minutes a day, out
loud, at your own pace. Nobody's listening but you (and the cat, who is asleep).

## Why this exists

I built Lilt for myself, because I'm terrible at discipline. The good stuff is all out there:
YouTube coaches, Reddit threads, voice guides, NHS leaflets. But it's spread across a hundred tabs,
and most nights I spent my practice time hunting for what to practise. So I pulled the pieces I
trusted into one place, put them in an order that builds slowly, and made it something I can open
and just start.

## Where I'm at

I'll be straight with you: I don't have the voice I'm working towards yet. Lilt is a structure I've
put together, not a method that's been proven to work, on me or on anyone. What it does give you is
a habit you can keep and an honest record. Every take is saved, so in a month you can listen back
and hear the change for yourself instead of guessing.

## How it works

- **Today** is a handful of cards. Say one, listen back, and tell Lilt how it felt. That decides
  when it comes back: _Again_ in a few minutes, _Hard_, _Good_ or _Easy_ a little later each time.
- **[Start here](#/studio/start-here)** first: set up your mic and record your "before".
- **Chapters** come in three parts. _Get ready_: setup and warm-ups. _The skills_: pitch,
  resonance, vocal weight, articulation and intonation, one at a time, each with an everyday phrase on
  the same visit. _Bring it together_: lists, phrases, reading, talking, laughs and little sounds,
  then real life. Stay until it feels easy; go back whenever. Not a speedrun.
- **Check-ins**: one text per chapter, same words every visit, so you can hear the glow-up.
- **Free recording**: just talk. You'll get your pitch line back.
- **The recorder** draws your pitch live with your comfy range behind it, and after a take shows your
  pitch and a rough comparison of your vowel resonances. The numbers describe a take; they cannot
  choose your voice or decide whether a change sounds better. Your comfort and listening lead.

## A small daily practice

You don't need to finish a chapter in a sitting. For an eight-minute visit, try this as a loose
budget, with room to stop earlier:

- **Settle and listen, about two minutes.** Try an easy warm-up, then hear one example or replay a
  take you like. Choose one thing to explore, such as a lighter sound or brighter resonance.
- **Lots of quick tries, about three minutes.** Record a familiar version, then five to ten short
  tries of one small change, on different words if you can. Many quick repetitions teach your body
  more than one careful one. Listen to a few: which do you prefer, and was it comfortable? If you
  cannot hear a useful difference, revisit the example or make the exercise easier.
- **Put it in words, about two minutes.** Try the same cue in "Hello, how are you?" or a phrase
  you actually use. This starts in the warm-up chapter; you don't have to unlock all the later
  chapters before practising a greeting.
- **Choose what to keep, about a minute.** Ask: was it comfortable, could I repeat it, and did I
  prefer the sound? Keep one useful cue for next time. "I'm not sure yet" is an honest answer.
- **Off the clock, once you're combining cues.** Talk to yourself in your practice voice for a couple
  of minutes while you do something else, and let those minutes grow slowly over the weeks. Using
  the voice, not just practising it, is what makes it yours.

These times are a way to organise a visit, not a required dose. Finish sooner if you want to.
Two or three short visits in a day may suit you better than one long one; there's no exact
evidence-backed amount, so let your voice tell you.
Today still introduces new cards gradually; the chapters are also there to browse at your pace.
Let meaning guide your melody: ordinary falls, rises and pauses all belong in speaking.

## Listening examples

The early skill chapters link to demonstrations by voice teachers, and NHS Fife has a
[warm-up demonstration](https://www.nhsfife.org/services/all-services/speech-and-language-therapy/how-to-take-care-of-your-voice/).
Listen for one contrast at a time, pause, then try a small comfortable version. The aim is to
understand the quality being explored, without having to copy the speaker's whole voice.

Dedicated A/B audio clips for these exercises are not available yet. Your own saved takes can
be personal references: replay a short version you like, then compare the next try with it.
If you cannot hear what a cue means, it is fine to stay with an easy exercise or ask a voice
professional for help. Bigger movements and higher numbers are not a substitute for understanding.

## Be gentle with your voice

It should never hurt. Stop if speaking hurts, feels strained or becomes hoarse, and rest instead
of pushing through. If symptoms persist, keep returning, or concern you, ask for help; don't wait.

- **See a GP or primary care doctor straight away** (in the US, ask for an ENT or laryngologist)
  for hoarseness that hasn't cleared in about three weeks, sudden voice loss, pain when you speak
  or swallow, or a lump in your neck. Coughing up blood or trouble breathing is urgent. ENT can
  look at your vocal folds.
- **After voice surgery,** follow your surgeon's rest plan and get the all-clear before using
  these exercises, including whispering.
- **Get checked before you start** if you already have a hoarse voice or reflux, smoke or vape,
  or use your voice a lot for work.
- **Notice the early signs.** Before anything hurts, a tight neck or jaw, an ache or "lump" in the
  throat, wanting to clear it, or a voice that's lower or rougher by the evening all mean: do less.
- **Build up slowly.** Practice is only part of your day's talking. If your voice is tired, sore or
  lower by the evening, do less tomorrow. Skip practice when you have a cold or have lost your voice.
- **Look after it.** Sip water through the day, keep the air from getting too dry, and go easy on
  alcohol, smoking and vaping. Swallow or sip instead of clearing your throat.

Some days your voice won't cooperate; that's normal, not a step back. Lilt is a practice tool, and
no clinician has reviewed this course.

## Hard days

Hearing your own voice can be hard, especially at the start. You don't have to listen to your
"before" recording until you want to. Plateaus and off days are part of it for everyone, and it's
fine to pause for a while and come back. If things feel heavy, talk to someone you trust; in the US
and Canada, [Trans Lifeline](https://translifeline.org) is run by and for trans people, and in the
UK, [Switchboard LGBT+](https://switchboard.lgbt) is there to listen.

## Working with a professional

Lilt is practice, not therapy. A speech and language therapist who works with gender-affirming
voice can hear what you can't, and many offer online sessions. In the UK, search
[ASLTIP](https://asltip.com); in the US, [ASHA ProFind](https://www.asha.org/profind/). If you're
considering voice surgery, therapists usually recommend voice training before and after it.

## What this course doesn't cover

Singing, and communication beyond the voice (gesture, posture, word choice), aren't here yet.
If you're deaf or hard of hearing, the pitch line can show whether your pitch moved. The resonance
comparison is rough and hasn't been tested on real voices, so it can't replace a listener; a
therapist, or someone you trust who can hear, can help with the rest.

## Make it better

Short articles about the world, each about a minute aloud: science, history, nature and your own voice. Reading facts asks for a different voice from a story: clear, curious and a little more even. They stay out of Today's cards.

**Read like a presenter.** Imagine you're explaining this to a friend who asked. Lean on the new or surprising word in each sentence, and let the rest be easy.

**Numbers and names.** Dates, figures and place names tend to pull a voice heavier or flatter. Keep them as light as the words around them.

**Questions to the listener.** Some articles ask "Have you ever…?" Let those sound like real questions, then settle back for the answer.

**One cue, the whole way.** Your note, brightness or a light feel: keep one from the headline thought to the final line, and reset at full stops if it drifts.

## sky-blue

Why is the sky blue? Sunlight looks white, but it's really a mixture of every colour of the rainbow. When that light travels through the air, it bumps into tiny molecules of gas, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. Those molecules scatter light in all directions, and they scatter the short, blue wavelengths far more than the long, red ones. So wherever you look in the sky, blue light is bouncing towards your eyes from every direction, and the whole dome above you glows blue. Violet light is scattered even more than blue, but our eyes are less sensitive to it, and some of it is absorbed high in the atmosphere. At sunset, the light has to travel through much more air to reach you. By then, most of the blue has been scattered away, and the oranges and reds are what's left to paint the clouds.

> Your check-in for this shelf: the same article each visit. Clear and curious, like explaining something you find genuinely interesting.

## your-voice

Every time you speak, two small folds of tissue in your throat, the vocal folds, come together and vibrate as air from your lungs passes between them. They're only a couple of centimetres long, yet in conversation they can open and close a couple of hundred times a second. That speed is what you hear as pitch. But the buzz they make on their own doesn't sound much like a voice. It's the space above them, your throat, your mouth and, for some sounds, your nose, that shapes the buzz into vowels and gives it its colour. Change the shape of that space by moving your tongue, your lips or your larynx, and the same buzz can sound brighter or darker. This is why two people with exactly the same pitch can sound completely different, and why a voice can change so much with practice.

> An article about the very thing you're practising. Read it like a teacher who loves the topic, and let "brighter or darker" sound like it.

## waggle-dance

When a honeybee finds a good patch of flowers, she flies home and tells the others where it is, by dancing. On the vertical honeycomb inside the dark hive, she runs a short straight line while waggling her body from side to side, then loops back round and does it again. The angle of that straight run, compared with straight up, shows the direction of the flowers compared with the direction of the sun. The longer she waggles, the further away the food is. Other bees crowd around, follow her movements, and then fly off to find the flowers for themselves. The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch spent years working out this language, and in nineteen seventy-three he shared a Nobel Prize for it. It's one of the most remarkable ways any animal is known to share information.

> A nature documentary voice: calm, warm, a little amazed. Keep the long explanation in the middle light and steady.

## octopus

The octopus is one of the strangest animals on Earth. It has three hearts: two pump blood through its gills, and the third sends it around the rest of the body. Its blood is blue, because it carries oxygen using copper rather than iron. And most of its nerve cells aren't in its head at all but in its eight arms, which can taste and touch and react almost on their own. Octopuses can change the colour and texture of their skin in a fraction of a second to vanish against rock or coral. In aquariums, some have been seen unscrewing jars, escaping from their tanks and squirting water at keepers they didn't like. Most species live only a year or two, which makes their cleverness all the more surprising. Every one of them works it all out alone.

> Full of surprising facts. Let each one land with a little lift of interest, and enjoy the funny bit about the keepers.

## sandwich

The sandwich is named after an English aristocrat, John Montagu, the fourth Earl of Sandwich, who lived in the eighteenth century. The story goes that he didn't want to leave the card table, or in another version his desk, so he asked for some meat to be brought to him between two slices of bread. That way he could eat with one hand and carry on with the other, without getting his fingers greasy. Other people began to order "the same as Sandwich", and the name stuck. Of course, people had been putting food between pieces of bread for a very long time before that. What the earl gave the world wasn't the idea but the name. Today you'll find versions of it almost everywhere, from a banh mi in Vietnam to a toastie in a café down the road.

> A light history piece with a legend in the middle. Let "the story goes" sound like you're sharing a good bit of gossip.

## penny-black

On the sixth of May, eighteen forty, Britain began using the world's first adhesive postage stamp: the Penny Black. It showed the profile of the young Queen Victoria on a black background, and it cost one penny to send a letter anywhere in the country. Before that, postage was usually paid by the person receiving the letter, and the price depended on the distance and the number of sheets. It was expensive and complicated, and people found clever ways to cheat, like hiding a message in the pattern of the address and then refusing to pay. The reformer Rowland Hill argued that a cheap, prepaid, flat rate would make the post simpler and more popular, and he was right. Within a few years, the number of letters sent had more than doubled, and countries around the world were copying the idea.

> History with dates and numbers. Keep the figures light, and let the cheating trick sound a little mischievous.

## tardigrades

If you could look closely at a damp patch of moss, you might find a tardigrade, a tiny animal often called a water bear. Under a microscope it looks like a chubby, eight-legged gummy bear, lumbering along on stubby claws. It's usually less than a millimetre long, but it's one of the toughest creatures known. When its surroundings dry out, a tardigrade can curl up into a dried-out ball and almost completely shut down. In that state, some have survived being frozen, heated, squeezed and blasted with radiation. In two thousand and seven, scientists even sent some into space on the outside of a satellite, and a number of them survived the trip. Add a drop of water, and the little ball slowly uncurls, stretches its legs and wanders off as if nothing had happened.

> A cheerful science story. Let the gummy bear picture make you smile, and keep the list of extremes bright rather than heavy.

## timbuktu

For centuries, the city of Timbuktu, at the edge of the Sahara in what is now Mali, was a great centre of learning. Scholars came there to study law, astronomy, medicine, mathematics and poetry, and families built up huge private libraries of handwritten manuscripts, some of them many hundreds of years old. These books were passed down carefully from parents to children and kept in wooden chests and mud-brick rooms. In twenty twelve, when armed groups took over the city, librarians and families feared the manuscripts would be destroyed. Many of them were quietly packed into metal trunks and smuggled south to the capital, Bamako, on donkey carts, in boats and in the backs of cars. Hundreds of thousands of pages were saved this way, thanks to people who decided that their history was worth the risk.

> A serious, admiring story. Keep your voice steady and clear through the place names, and let the ending carry quiet respect.

## purring

How a cat purrs is still a bit of a puzzle. Most scientists agree it starts in the larynx, the same part of the throat that we use to speak. The usual explanation is that tiny muscles there twitch rapidly, opening and closing the space between the vocal folds, so the air rumbles as the cat breathes both in and out. That's why a purr can carry on without a break, unlike a meow. But in two thousand and twenty-three, researchers found that a cat's larynx, given a flow of air, can make purring sounds even without signals from the brain, helped by small pads of soft tissue in its vocal folds. What a purr means is even more interesting. Cats purr when they're relaxed and happy, but also when they're hungry, nervous or unwell, and mother cats purr to their kittens, who can't yet see or hear well but can feel the vibration. So a purr seems to be less "I'm happy" and more "everything's all right here", whether that's true yet or not.

> A cosy little science piece, perfect for the cat in the room. Gentle and curious, with a soft smile at the end.

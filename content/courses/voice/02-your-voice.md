Your voice does a lot for you every day, not just when you practise. This page gathers the voice care from across the course into one place, to come back to whenever you like. It's the same whichever way you're taking your voice: higher, lower or still open.

**Easy is the goal, in any direction.** Every change in this course should feel easy. Higher never means squeezed or pinched, lower never means pushed down or pressed, and fuller never means louder or rougher. If a change only works by forcing it, make it smaller, or leave it for another day.

**Warm up, then cool down.** A minute or two of [Warm-ups](#/studio/warmup) gets your voice moving before you ask anything new of it: a hum, a lip trill or an "ooo" through a straw. At the end of a visit, the [cool-down](#/studio/warmup/cool-down) slides a hum down into a sigh. Then have some water.

**Short and often is enough.** Five to ten minutes makes a good visit, and two or three short visits may suit you better than one long one. Practice is only part of the talking you do in a day, so on a day full of calls, meetings or a loud evening out, do less, or just warm up and cool down. There's no amount you have to reach.

**Keep it watered.** Your vocal folds work best when they're moist. Keep a drink beside you while you practise and sip through the day; NHS Lothian suggests about one and a half litres of drinks without caffeine or alcohol. Dry, heated rooms dry your throat too: a bowl of water near the radiator, a humidifier, or a few breaths of warm steam can help.

**Swallow instead of clearing.** Clearing your throat or coughing when there's nothing to bring up is hard on your voice, and it easily becomes a habit. When the urge comes, sip some water, swallow firmly, or breathe in through your nose and out through gently pursed lips.

**Rest when it's tired.** Short quiet breaks through a busy talking day help. With a cold, a sore throat or a voice that's gone, skip practice and talk as little as you comfortably can. Don't swap to whispering: it can strain a sore voice too, so speak softly and briefly, or write it down, and leave the whispered exercises for another day.

**Go easy on noise.** Shouting, or talking over music, traffic or a crowd, tires a voice quickly. Get closer to the person, catch their eye, or move somewhere quieter instead of raising your voice.

**What else touches your voice.** Smoke irritates the vocal folds, so smoking, vaping and smoky rooms are worth keeping away from it. Reflux can reach your throat, especially at night: smaller meals, nothing to eat in the three hours before bed, and raising the head of your bed can help. Some medicines dry the throat, inhalers among them: rinse your mouth and sip water after a steroid inhaler, and ask your doctor if a prescription seems to affect your voice rather than stopping it yourself. Stress and tiredness show in the voice too, so a slow breath and a bit of rest count as voice care.

**Notice the early signs.** Before anything hurts, your voice often warns you: a tight neck or jaw, an ache or "lump" in the throat, wanting to clear it, top notes that have gone missing, a voice that's rougher or weaker by the evening, or talking that starts to feel like work. Any of these means: stop for today, sip some water and rest. Come back another day with an easy hum. If you take testosterone, voice breaks and a tired, scratchy voice are common while it changes; treat those days the same way.

**Stop straight away if it hurts.** Pain, a voice that keeps cracking or cutting out, or losing your voice all mean stop, not push through. In Today, **It hurts or feels strained** pauses your cards until speaking feels easy again.

**When to see someone.** If a hoarse voice hasn't started getting better after about two weeks, or voice problems keep coming back, see a GP or primary care doctor. Don't wait if hoarseness hasn't cleared in three weeks, or if you lose your voice suddenly, it hurts to speak or swallow, or you find a lump in your neck; in the US, ask for an ENT or laryngologist, who can look at your vocal folds. Trouble breathing, or coughing up blood, is an emergency. After voice surgery, follow your surgeon's rest plan and get the all-clear before practising, whispering included.

**Someone to help.** A speech and language therapist who specialises in voice can hear what you can't, and help when practice keeps feeling hard, stuck or uncomfortable. The Course guide on [All chapters](#/studio/chapters) says how to find one.

**A gentle check-in.** The questions below are for noticing, not scoring. Answer one out loud before you practise, or just in your head. "Fine" and "not sure" are good answers, and so is "too tired to talk today".

## feel-today

How does your voice feel today?

> Your check-in: a sentence or two, in whatever voice comes. Easy, tired, scratchy, lower than usual? If talking feels like work today, answer in your head instead and rest.

## yesterday

How did your voice feel at the end of yesterday?

> Think back to the evening. If it was rougher, weaker or quieter by then, it had a long day: ask a little less of it today.

## throat

How does your throat feel right now?

> Dry, tight, achy, or comfortable? If it's dry, have a few sips of water before you start.

## easy

What feels easy for your voice today, and what doesn't?

> Name one of each. Start your practice with the easy one.

## talking-ahead

How much talking is ahead of you today?

> Calls, meetings or a noisy evening already ask a lot of your voice. On days like that, a warm-up and a cool-down can be all the practice you need.

## rest

When will your voice get a break today?

> Pick one quiet moment, like the walk home or the half hour after a long call. A few minutes without talking count.

## helps

What helps your voice feel good?

> Water, a hum, a quiet hour, a warm drink, a slow breath? Notice what works for you, so you can come back to it on harder days.

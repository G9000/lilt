Vocal weight is how thick or thin your voice sounds: how much of your vocal folds meets as they vibrate. More contact sounds thicker and fuller; less sounds thinner and lighter. Weight, pitch and resonance are separate dials, but listeners often hear a fuller voice as lower even when the pitch hasn't moved. Voices tend to thicken in their lower notes and thin out higher up; you can learn to keep the fullness as your pitch moves. On testosterone, a thicker sound gets easier to reach. Fuller doesn't mean louder, pressed or creaky: aim for full, clear and easy.

**Hear thin and full.** Say "uh-oh" the way you would to a toddler: thin and light. Then a friendly "HEY!" to someone across the street, at about the same pitch (a call, not a shout). Notice the thicker buzz on "HEY". That thickness is weight.

**Borrow a full sound.** Some little sounds are full on their own: a satisfied "mm!" after a good bite, a thinking "hmm", a relaxed "uh-huh". Say one, then say the next word with the same feel.

**Let breath carry it.** A fuller voice needs a steady stream of breath under it, not a push from the throat. Take a slow, low breath, start the sound as you begin to breathe out, and pause for a new breath before you run short.

**Not pressed, not creaky, not breathy.** Pressed sounds tight and effortful, as if lifting something heavy. Creak is a crackly, popping rattle, often at the ends of phrases. Breathy is airy and hard to hear. None of them is fuller. If you hear one, ease off, breathe, and go back to an easy "mm".

**Keep it full when your pitch rises.** Excitement, laughing or a surprised question can lift your pitch and thin your voice. That's normal, and you don't need to stop your pitch moving: practise keeping a little of the fullness as it rises, on the lines you actually say in those moments.

**Use it in words.** "Yeah, that sounds good to me." with the full feel from the very first sound. If it starts thin, say "mm" first and go straight into it.

**Listen back.** Lilt doesn't measure weight, so this one is your ears. Play your everyday take and your fuller take back to back: the fuller one should sound thicker and buzzier, still clear and easy to follow, and it shouldn't feel like work.

**Keep it gentle.** Full is easy, never squeezed. If your throat tightens, your voice goes rough or croaky, or you feel you're pushing, stop and rest, then come back with an easy hum. The "HEY" is only for hearing the contrast: never shout it.

## contrast

uh-oh · hey!

> Toddler "uh-oh", then a friendly "hey!" across the street, at about the same pitch and an ordinary volume. Listen for the thicker buzz on "hey". Never shout it.

## full-mm

mm! · hmm · uh-huh

> A satisfied "mm!", a thinking "hmm", a relaxed "uh-huh". Notice how full they are with no effort: that's the feel to carry into words.

## full-ah

aaah · aaah · aaah

> Three easy ahs at about the same pitch and loudness: your usual, a fuller one, then the fuller one again. Louder isn't fuller, so keep the volume the same.

## breath-count

hhh… one, two, three, four, five.

> A slow, low breath, then count as you breathe out, full and easy on every number. Pause for a new breath before you run short rather than squeezing out the last one.

## full-hey

mm… hey there.

> Your check-in: an "mm" as full and easy as feels right today, flowing into "hey there". Next time, play it beside this one.

## full-phrase

Yeah, that sounds good to me.

> Bring the full, clear feel into the sentence from the first sound. If it turns pressed or creaky, ease off and take a breath; if it turns thin, start with "mm".

## excited

No way! That's brilliant news!

> Excited, and let your pitch move with it. Then again, keeping a little of the fullness as it rises. You don't have to hold your pitch down.

## full-question

Wait, are you serious?

> A surprised question tends to go up and thin out. Let it rise, and keep some of the full feel underneath.

A shelf of longer things to read aloud: stories, letters, a recipe, a weather report, a job interview. They aren't in Today's cards; come here when you want a longer stretch, or when the short lines start to feel too easy.

**Pick one cue.** Your note, brightness or a light feel: choose one for the whole passage, and add a second only once the first holds to the end.

**Read in pieces.** A passage is about a minute aloud. Read a few sentences, pause, and carry on. A take stops by itself after two minutes, so there's no need to rush.

**Reset at every full stop.** A breath at the end of a sentence is a free chance to find your note again before the next one starts.

**Let the writing lead.** Each passage has its own mood: chatty, calm, dramatic, businesslike. Let your melody follow it, the way you would reading to a friend.

**Listen to the end.** Most voices drift back in the last few lines. Compare the first sentence with the last, and next time reset a little sooner.

## morning-walk

Most mornings I take the long way to the bus stop, past the bakery and along the canal. The water is usually still at that hour, and the ducks are already arguing about something. A man in a green coat walks his old dog there every day, and we nod to each other like colleagues. Halfway along there is a bench with a small brass plaque that says "For Margaret, who loved this view." I don't know who Margaret was, but I think she had good taste. By the time I reach the bridge, the city has started to wake up properly: shutters rattling open, a radio somewhere, the smell of coffee drifting out of a doorway. I like arriving at the stop a few minutes early, just to stand there and listen to it all begin.

> Your check-in for this shelf: the same calm passage each visit, so you can hear your longer reading change over time. Keep one cue from the first line to the last.

## bakery

The bell above the door jingled as Nora stepped inside, shaking the rain off her hood. "Morning!" called Mr Okafor from behind the counter. "The usual?" She laughed. "Am I that predictable?" "One cinnamon bun and a very large tea," he said, already reaching for the tongs. "You're not predictable. You're reliable. There's a difference." Nora leaned on the counter and watched him wrap the bun in paper. The shop was warm and smelled of butter and sugar, and the windows had fogged up so the street outside looked like a watercolour. "Any news?" she asked. "My granddaughter starts school on Monday," he said, beaming. "She has already chosen her lunchbox. It has a dinosaur on it. A pink one." "A pink dinosaur," Nora said. "Excellent choice." She paid, wished him luck, and stepped back out into the rain, smiling.

> Two voices in one story. Let the dialogue sound like real people talking, and keep your own voice underneath both of them.

## umbrella

I have lost another umbrella. That makes four this year, and it's only October. The first one I left on a train, which is fair enough; everybody does that. The second blew inside out on a bridge and I gave it a quiet funeral in a bin. The third, I honestly don't know. One day it was by the door and the next day it simply wasn't. Did someone borrow it? Did it leave of its own accord? I'll never know. And today, the fourth one stayed behind in a café while I walked home in the drizzle feeling oddly free. So here is my question: is it really worth buying a fifth? Or should I accept that I am, at heart, a person who gets a little bit wet, and make my peace with it?

> Chatty and a little dramatic. Let the questions lift where they feel like real questions, and enjoy the comic timing.

## voice-note

Hey, it's me! Sorry, I know you're probably busy, so I'll be quick. Well, I'll try to be quick. So, you know how I said I'd look at flats this weekend? I went to see that one near the park, the one with the yellow door, and honestly? I think I love it. The kitchen is tiny, like really tiny, but there's a window above the sink that looks straight out onto a tree, and the light in the morning is just gorgeous. The landlady was lovely too. She has two cats and she showed me photos of both of them before she showed me the bathroom, which tells you everything. Anyway, I'm going to call the agent tomorrow. Can you come and see it with me on Thursday? I'd love a second opinion. Okay, that's it. Call me back when you can. Bye!

> Like a real voice message to a friend: quick, warm and a bit rambly. Keep your voice there through the asides and the "anyway".

## lemon-cake

You will need two hundred grams of soft butter, two hundred grams of caster sugar, four eggs, two hundred grams of self-raising flour, and two lemons. Start by heating the oven to one hundred and eighty degrees and lining a loaf tin with baking paper. Beat the butter and sugar together until the mixture is pale and fluffy; this takes a few minutes, so be patient. Add the eggs one at a time, mixing well after each one. Grate the zest of both lemons into the bowl, then fold in the flour gently, so you keep the air in. Spoon the mixture into the tin and smooth the top. Bake for about forty-five minutes, until a skewer comes out clean. While it is still warm, squeeze the lemon juice over the top with a spoonful of sugar. Let it cool, if you can wait.

> Clear and friendly, like you're teaching someone in your kitchen. Numbers are a good test: keep them as light as the rest.

## letter

Dear future me, I'm writing this on a quiet Sunday afternoon with a cup of tea going cold beside me. I don't know exactly where you are as you read this, or what has changed, but I hope you're being kind to yourself. Right now I'm learning a lot of new things at once, and some days it feels slow. I want you to remember that slow is still moving. I hope you kept the habits that made you feel steady: the morning walks, the music while cooking, calling the people who make you laugh. I hope you still notice small good things, like the first warm day of the year or a stranger holding a door. If something I'm worried about today turned out fine, please smile about it for me. And if it didn't, I hope you found your way through anyway. With love, from me, a little while ago.

> Gentle and personal. Let it be warm rather than performed, and keep the ends of sentences easy as they fall.

## announcement

Good evening, ladies and gentlemen, and welcome aboard the seven fifteen service to Brighton, calling at Clapham Junction, East Croydon, Gatwick Airport and Haywards Heath. We're sorry for the short delay this evening; this was caused by a signal fault just outside the station, which has now been fixed. The buffet car is located in coach C, towards the middle of the train, and is serving hot and cold drinks and a selection of snacks. Please keep your belongings with you at all times, and let a member of staff know if you see anything unusual. We expect to arrive at Brighton at eight twenty-six. On behalf of everyone on board, thank you for travelling with us tonight, and we hope you have a pleasant journey.

> A public announcement: clear, calm and a little formal, aimed at a whole carriage. Open your mouth a touch more rather than pushing louder.

## weather

And now the weather. It's been a grey and blustery day across most of the country, with heavy showers in the west and some hail over the hills this afternoon. Tonight those showers will gradually ease, and by midnight most places will be dry, though it will feel chilly, with temperatures dropping to around four degrees in towns and close to freezing in sheltered valleys. Tomorrow starts bright and crisp, with plenty of sunshine for the morning commute. Cloud will build from the south-west during the afternoon, and there's a chance of rain reaching the coast by early evening. Winds will be lighter than today, so it should feel a little kinder overall. Looking further ahead, the weekend is set to turn milder, with highs of thirteen degrees by Sunday. That's all from me. Have a lovely evening.

> A broadcaster's voice: steady pace, clear consonants, a friendly sign-off. Watch for the pitch sinking during the long middle stretch.

## museum

If you'd like to follow me, we'll start here, in front of the largest painting in the room. It was finished in eighteen seventy-two, and it shows a harbour at dawn. Take a moment to look at the light on the water. Can you see how the artist has used tiny dabs of pink and gold rather than one smooth colour? Up close, it looks almost messy, but if you step back a few paces, the whole surface seems to shimmer. Critics at the time hated it. One newspaper called it "a smudge with ambitions". Within twenty years, though, the same style was being taught in every art school in Europe. Now, look at the little boat on the left. That figure in the red scarf is thought to be the artist's sister, who rowed out every morning so he could paint. Shall we move on to the next room?

> Explaining to a group: point things out with your voice, lean on the words that matter, and make the questions sound inviting.

## lighthouse

The storm arrived just after midnight. At first it was only a low moan around the walls of the lighthouse, but within an hour the wind was screaming, and waves were throwing spray as high as the lamp itself. Elsie climbed the spiral stairs with a lantern, counting every step. One hundred and twelve. At the top, the great lens was turning steadily, sweeping its beam across the black water. And there, caught for a heartbeat in the light, was a small fishing boat, tossed sideways and far too close to the rocks. Elsie didn't hesitate. She grabbed the old brass horn and blew three long blasts into the night. For a terrible moment, nothing happened. Then, slowly, the little boat turned, pointed its nose towards the open sea, and pulled away from the rocks. Elsie sat down on the cold floor, shaking, and laughed out loud.

> A story with suspense. Let your pace and melody build towards the boat and the rocks, then relax at the end, without pushing your voice.

## interview

Thank you for having me. So, a little about myself: I've worked in customer support for about six years, first in a busy call centre and more recently leading a small team of five. What I enjoy most is solving problems that seem complicated at first but turn out to have a simple fix once you really listen to the person. In my current role, I introduced a weekly session where the team shares the trickiest calls of the week, and that cut our repeat complaints by almost a third. Outside work, I volunteer at a community garden on Saturdays, which keeps me humble, mostly because the tomatoes never do what I tell them. I'm applying for this position because I'd love to work somewhere that values patience and clear communication as much as I do.

> Professional and warm, like a real interview. Keep your voice steady and confident through the figures and the little joke.

## night-market

On Friday nights the car park behind the library turns into a market. By seven o'clock it's full of lights strung between the lampposts, and the air is thick with the smell of grilled corn, spiced noodles and sugary doughnuts. There's a stall that sells nothing but socks with foxes on them, and a woman who paints your name on a grain of rice. Somebody is always playing a guitar near the fountain, slightly out of tune but very enthusiastic. Children run between the tables with glow sticks, and older couples sit on the library steps sharing a paper tray of dumplings. I usually walk the whole way round twice before deciding what to eat, and then I buy the same thing as last week anyway: a mango drink, a bag of hot chestnuts, and far too many sesame balls.

> Full of lists and little pictures. Let each item sound interesting, and keep the brightness through the long sentences.

## austen

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife. However little known the feelings or views of such a man may be on his first entering a neighbourhood, this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families, that he is considered the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters. "My dear Mr. Bennet," said his lady to him one day, "have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?" Mr. Bennet replied that he had not. "But it is," returned she; "for Mrs. Long has just been here, and she told me all about it." Mr. Bennet made no answer.

> The opening of Pride and Prejudice: long, elegant sentences and a dry sense of humour. Pause at the commas, and let Mrs. Bennet's excitement come through.

## alice

Alice was beginning to get very tired of sitting by her sister on the bank, and of having nothing to do: once or twice she had peeped into the book her sister was reading, but it had no pictures or conversations in it, "and what is the use of a book," thought Alice, "without pictures or conversations?" So she was considering in her own mind (as well as she could, for the hot day made her feel very sleepy and stupid), whether the pleasure of making a daisy-chain would be worth the trouble of getting up and picking the daisies, when suddenly a White Rabbit with pink eyes ran close by her.

> The opening of Alice in Wonderland: one very long sentence, then a surprise. Breathe where the commas let you, and let the rabbit arrive with a little lift.

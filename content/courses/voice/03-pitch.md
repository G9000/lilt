Pitch is how high or low your voice sounds. It is one important part of a lighter voice, alongside resonance and vocal weight. There is no single pitch that makes a voice light. Explore a speaking range you like and can use comfortably, rather than aiming for the highest note you can reach.

**How it fits together.** Your vocal folds make the sound: how fast they vibrate is pitch, and how thick and firmly they meet is weight. Your throat, mouth and nose then shape that sound: that's resonance. The next chapters take one each, so you can learn to change them separately.

**Find where you are now.** Record a few everyday sentences. The middle pitch describes that sample, not your whole range. A relaxed "mm-hm!" can be a useful way into sound, but its pitch may differ from your usual speaking voice.

**Explore a small change.** Try a short, gentle hum glide within an easy range, then say "Hello, how are you?" near a spot you liked. Compare it with your everyday version. A useful starting place feels easy in words as well as in the hum; it does not need to be a set number of semitones higher. Stop if speaking feels strained or becomes hoarse.

**If your voice flips.** A glide may move into a different register. A flip does not by itself mean injury or failure, and register labels do not decide what your voice should be. Return to an easy sound if the change is difficult to control. A voice-specialist speech and language therapist can help if you keep getting stuck or uncomfortable.

**Anchor it.** Hum your note, then open the hum straight into counting. The hum gives you the note; the counting turns it into speech.

**Progress by ease, not a calendar.** There is no weekly pitch increase to complete. Keep a range when you like it and can repeat it comfortably in a short phrase. Change only one thing next: the sound, the phrase length, or the situation. You may spend time on resonance or weight without raising pitch further.

**Let it move.** Speech isn't singing one note. Start your sentence near your note and let it rise and fall with what you mean. Holding one note the whole way sounds more like singing than talking.

**Listen back.** Does it sound like you, and could you talk like this for a few minutes? Once you know your note, save the range around it as your [comfortable range](#/studio/settings) so the recorder shows it behind your pitch line.

## mm-hm

mm-hm!

> Agree with an imaginary friend. Notice the easy start and the changing pitch. This is an exploration, not a measurement of your everyday speaking pitch.

## hum-speak

mmm

> Hum on an easy note. Compare its feel with a short spoken phrase; the hum is a starting cue, not a target to hold throughout speech.

## glide

mmm ↗ ↘

> Make a small, comfortable slide up and back down. Choose an easy spot somewhere within it and try a greeting there. You are exploring options, not finding your highest note. Stop for strain or hoarseness.

## hum-target

mmmmmm

> Hum your note (the easy spot you found on the glide) for a few seconds. A small smile brightens the sound; it doesn't change the note. If your throat tightens, take it down a step. Happy with it? Save the range around it in Practice settings.

## hum-into-count

mmm… one, two, three, four, five.

> Hum your note, then open it into counting without a gap. Keep the numbers sounding like speech, not singing.

## count-5

One, two, three, four, five.

> Your check-in: hum somewhere easy (your note, once you've found it), then count. Let the numbers move a little, like normal speech.

## easy-hello

Hello, how are you?

> Start near your note (or anywhere easy, if you haven't found it yet) and let the greeting go where it wants. "How are you?" can rise, fall, or both.

## count-20

One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty.

> A longer run. Pitch tends to sink as you go; take a breath and hum your note again whenever it does.

Three short stories, each told in parts of about a minute. Read one part a visit, or a whole story when you have the time and the voice for it. They stay out of Today's cards.

**Read like a storyteller.** Stories carry you along, which makes them a good way to keep your voice going for longer without thinking about every word. Let the plot set your pace.

**Give people their own voices, gently.** When a character speaks, change your melody or pace a little rather than your pitch or weight. Your practised voice stays underneath all of them.

**One part, one cue.** Choose the one thing you're keeping (your note, brightness or a light feel) before you press record, and check the end of the part against the start.

**Pick up where you left off.** Each part starts where the last one stopped, so you can come back tomorrow and carry on.

## night-bus-1

The number forty-two was the last bus of the night, and Rosa had driven it for eleven years. She knew every pothole on the route and every regular passenger by their stop. There was the nurse who got on at the hospital at ten past midnight and fell asleep against the window. There were the two cooks from the noodle bar, still smelling of ginger, arguing cheerfully about football. And there was the man who never said a word, who sat at the very back and got off by the river. On the first cold night of November, a new passenger climbed aboard at the stop outside the music college. She was young, with a cello case almost as big as she was, and she tapped her card, apologised to nobody in particular, and wedged herself and the cello into the seat behind Rosa. Then, very quietly, she began to hum.

> Part one of three. Set the scene calmly, like the start of a bedtime story, and let each passenger have their own little moment.

## night-bus-2

At first Rosa thought it was the heater, which had a habit of whistling. But it was a tune, low and slow, and it seemed to fill the whole bus without being loud at all. The nurse opened her eyes. The two cooks stopped arguing. Even the silent man at the back lifted his head. "Sorry," the girl said, when she noticed Rosa watching her in the mirror. "I've got an exam tomorrow. I hum when I'm nervous." "Don't stop on my account," said Rosa. "What is it?" "It's from a piece I have to play. I can never get the middle part right." She hummed it again, and this time, from the back of the bus, a deep voice joined in. It was the silent man. He knew every note, and when the girl stumbled in the middle, he carried the tune for her until she found it again.

> Part two. Two people meet through a song. Let the dialogue be natural and quiet, as if you don't want to wake anyone.

## night-bus-3

They hummed it together all the way to the river, the girl and the man, while the rest of the bus listened. When they reached his stop, he stood up, tipped an imaginary hat to her, and said, "Lift the bow a little earlier in the middle. You're holding your breath." Then he was gone into the dark. The girl stared after him. "Who was that?" she asked. Rosa shrugged. "Eleven years, and that's the first thing I've heard him say." The next night, the cello case was back, and so was the girl, grinning from ear to ear. "I passed," she announced to the whole bus, and the cooks cheered. The silent man was in his usual seat. He didn't say anything this time, but when the girl started humming, he was the first to join in, and before long, half the bus was humming along.

> The end of the story. Let the ending feel warm, with a little lift for "I passed", and keep your voice easy through to the last word.

## repair-cafe-1

On the first Saturday of every month, the community hall on Elm Street turns into a repair café. People bring in whatever is broken, a lamp, a jumper, a toaster, a bicycle with a sulky chain, and volunteers fix it for free while everyone drinks tea out of mismatched mugs. Priya had volunteered there for two years, mostly mending clothes, so she was surprised when the organiser waved her over to the electronics table. "Kwame's off sick," he said, "and this lady has been waiting for an hour." The lady was small and very upright, with a silver brooch shaped like a bird. On the table in front of her sat an old wooden radio, the kind with a big round dial and a cloth-covered speaker. "It was my husband's," she said. "It stopped working the week after he died. I'd like to hear it one more time."

> Part one of three. A gentle, everyday opening. Let the list of broken things bounce along, then slow down for the lady and her radio.

## repair-cafe-2

Priya was not an expert in radios. She was an expert in buttons, hems and stubborn zips. But the lady looked at her so hopefully that she rolled up her sleeves and found a screwdriver. The back panel came off with a puff of dust that smelled like an attic. Inside, the radio was a little city of glass valves, coloured wires and paper tubes. "My husband used to say it took a minute to warm up," the lady said, "like him in the mornings." Priya laughed, and the lady laughed too. They worked together, with the lady holding the torch and Priya peering at each connection. After twenty minutes, she found it: a single wire that had come loose from the volume knob, its end blackened and frayed. "That might be all it is," Priya said carefully. "I don't want to promise anything." "Try," said the lady.

> Part two. A quiet, careful scene. Let the funny line about the mornings land, and keep the tension soft as they search.

## repair-cafe-3

Priya trimmed the wire, borrowed a soldering iron from the bicycle table, and with steadier hands than she expected, fixed it back into place. She screwed on the panel, plugged in the radio and turned the dial. Nothing happened. The lady's face didn't change, but her hands tightened on her handbag. Then, slowly, the dial began to glow a warm orange, and a crackle rose from the speaker, and then a voice, mid-sentence, reading the shipping forecast in a calm, low tone. "Fisher, German Bight, westerly five or six, occasionally seven." The lady closed her eyes. "He listened to this every night," she said, "though he never went near a boat in his life." All around the hall, people had stopped what they were doing to listen. When the forecast ended, someone started clapping, and then everyone was. The lady shook Priya's hand. "Thank you," she said. "Same time next month?"

> The end. A moment of silence, then the radio, then applause. Let your voice carry the feeling without pushing, and leave a little pause before "Thank you".

## sound-collector-1

When Mei was nine, her grandmother gave her an old pocket recorder and said, "Go and collect the sounds that matter." Mei took this very seriously. She recorded the creak of the third stair, the one everyone stepped over at night. She recorded rain on the kitchen skylight, the neighbour's dog sneezing, and her little brother trying to whistle and producing only a damp sort of hiss. She labelled each one carefully in a notebook: "Stair, Tuesday." "Rain, heavy." "Dog, surprised." By the end of the summer she had sixty-four sounds. Her favourite was her grandmother laughing at a joke on the radio, a laugh that started low and climbed higher and higher until it turned into a little squeak at the top. Mei played it back so often that her grandmother began to complain. "You'll wear me out," she said, but she was smiling.

> Part one of four. A child's world, full of small, funny sounds. Enjoy the labels in the notebook, and let the laugh at the end climb a little.

## sound-collector-2

Years later, when Mei moved to a new city for university, the recorder came with her in a shoebox, wrapped in a scarf. The city was loud in a way she wasn't used to: sirens, buses, people shouting into phones in five languages. For the first few weeks she felt small and tired and very far from home. One night, unable to sleep, she took out the recorder and pressed play at random. Out came the third stair, creaking. Then the rain on the skylight. Then the dog, sneezing. She laughed out loud in her tiny room, and the girl next door knocked on the wall. The next morning Mei did something she hadn't done in years. She took the recorder out into the street and started collecting again: the bakery's bell, the clatter of a tram, a busker's accordion wheezing out of tune.

> Part two. Lonely at first, then a turn towards hope. Let the mood shift in your melody when she laughs out loud.

## sound-collector-3

The girl next door turned out to be called Inès, and she wanted to know why Mei was laughing at midnight. So Mei showed her the shoebox and the notebook full of labels. Inès read them out slowly, delighted. "Dog, surprised. Brother, whistling, unsuccessful." She looked up. "Can I add one?" Mei handed her the recorder. Inès thought for a moment, then held it to the window and recorded the city at two in the morning: a distant train, a pigeon complaining on the ledge, someone singing badly on their way home. She labelled it in her own handwriting: "Night, new friend." After that, they went collecting together. They recorded the river under the old bridge, the echo in the underground car park, the hiss of the espresso machine at the café where Inès worked. The notebook filled up faster than it ever had before.

> Part three. Friendship growing. Read Inès's labels with a little sparkle, and keep the long list light and bright.

## sound-collector-4

In the spring, Mei's grandmother came to visit. She was slower on the stairs now and her eyes weren't as sharp, but her laugh was exactly the same. On her last evening, Mei and Inès made her a gift: a recording of every sound they had collected in the city, one after another, with their own voices in between explaining what each one was. Her grandmother listened to the whole thing in silence, holding the little recorder close to her ear. When it finished, she was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "You forgot one." She pressed record, held it up between the three of them, and told the worst joke Mei had ever heard. They all laughed, and her grandmother's laugh climbed higher and higher until it turned into a little squeak at the top. Mei didn't need to label that one. She already knew what it was called.

> The end of the story. Let the quiet moments be quiet, and the laughter at the end light and bright. Notice whether your voice is the same at the last line as at the first.

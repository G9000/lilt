Practice in a quiet room is one thing; a noisy café or a phone call is another. This chapter rehearses the moments you'll actually meet, so they feel familiar when they come.

**Climb a ladder.** A voice note to yourself, then one to a friend, then ordering something, then a phone call to a shop. Move up a rung when the one below feels ordinary, and step back down whenever you like. Rehearse each line here first, with the cues you can combine (see "Add a second cue" in Automatic speech). Count the minutes you use your practice voice off the clock (see Automatic speech) and let them grow slowly. If your voice is tired or low by the evening, use it a little less tomorrow.

**Phones are harder.** The line is tinnier and there's no face to help, so voices often drop back. Start calls with a line you've rehearsed, and take your time.

**When you're tired or upset.** Tiredness, stress and strong feelings pull voices back to their everyday setting. That's normal. Practise a few lines with real feeling behind them, and on tired days let your voice be easier rather than forcing it.

**Being heard.** In a noisy place, get closer or step away from the noise when you can, then aim your words at the person and open your mouth a little more. Getting louder from your throat makes the voice heavier and tires it. [Being heard](#/studio/being-heard) practises this on its own.

**Borrow an ear.** Once a month, if you'd like, send someone you trust two unlabelled takes of the same line, an old one and a new one, and ask which sounds more like the voice you described. One opinion, not a verdict. Think twice before posting recordings on public forums: they can be saved and shared.

**Keep it.** There's no deadline, so give yourself time. When this is simply your voice, keep one check-in a week and your before-and-afters. If one drifts, go back to that skill's chapter for a while.

**Come back after.** Record the line again, or just say how it went. Did it feel easy, and did it feel like you? Someone else's reaction is only one piece of information.

## answer-phone

Hello, Alex speaking. How can I help?

> Your check-in: use your own name. Is your voice there from "Hello", or does it settle in later?

## flat-white

Hi! Could I get an oat flat white, please? Small is fine.

> Your real order, if you'd rather. Friendly and ordinary, like you do it every day.

## appointment

I'd like to book an appointment for Thursday afternoon, if you have one.

> Clear and calm. Getting the message across matters more than holding an exact note.

## station

Sorry, could you tell me how to get to the station from here?

> Asking a stranger: polite, a little brighter, and your question can end however feels natural.

## call-friend

Hey! I just wanted to hear your voice. Have you got a minute to chat?

> Rehearse the opening. Making the actual call is up to you, whenever you're ready.

## lovely-day

Thank you so much, you've been really helpful. Have a lovely day!

> A warm goodbye. It's fine for "day" to fall.

## voice-note

Hey, it's me! Just a quick voice note to say I'll be there at seven.

> Send it to yourself as a real voice note, then play it back on your phone's speaker. Phones squash the sound; does your voice still come through?

## not-okay

Honestly, I'm a bit frustrated. This is the third time I've asked.

> Say it with real feeling, not shouting. Notice whether your voice holds or drops back, and keep it easy on your throat.

## across-the-room

Excuse me! Sorry, is this seat taken?

> In a busy room, take a few steps closer first if you can, then aim the words at the person and open your mouth more, rather than pushing from your throat. Keep it short.

## caught-off-guard

Oh! Sorry, what was that?

> Say it fast, as if someone spoke to you from behind. Was your voice there from the very first "Oh"? If not, that's useful to know.
